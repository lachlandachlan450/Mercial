Research Fellowship

A month working on an open problem in AI safety with a facilitator, ending in a write-up for the Mercial blog. Each facilitator runs a stream of problems they have chosen to supervise. You apply to a stream and tell us which of its problems you would be excited to work on.

Research Fellowship 2 runs 1 November – 1 December. Applications are open until Wednesday 28 October. Offers go out shortly after the deadline.

How it works

  1. Pick a stream

    Read the streams below. Each problem expands to show the full brief: the question, what you need coming in, and what you will have produced by the end.

  2. Apply to it

    One short form per stream. You tell us your background, how much you have interacted with AI safety, rank the problems in that stream, and confirm you have the weekly capacity. Apply to more than one stream if you like.

  3. Have a chat

    We invite you to an informal conversation, mostly to gauge fit and answer your questions.

  4. Join the cohort

    Facilitators read applications to their stream and make picks; we resolve any overlaps so each applicant gets at most one offer. You will hear from us either way.

Streams

Technical experience is not required for the fellowship — some streams are conceptual. Compute funding is available for the ones that need it. If you are unsure whether you are ready, err on the side of applying.

Jack Lakkapragada

Independent researcher · SPAR fellow

Technical
Why do agents collude? Work out what leads AI agents to coordinate outside their intended task boundaries.

The problem. We've seen AI agents use unexpected places to communicate and coordinate during tasks, including cases like DSEWiki. But what actually leads them to do this?

By collusion, we mean agents coordinating outside their intended task boundaries, such as sharing information they weren't supposed to share or using unexpected communication channels. Not all agent communication is collusion, and that's an important distinction we want to investigate.

In this project, we'll look at real agent interaction logs and investigate two main questions:

  1. What kinds of tasks or situations lead agents to collude?
  2. Why do agents choose particular places or channels to communicate?

We'll reconstruct specific cases, separate what the logs actually show from what we're inferring, and look for patterns across different tasks and environments. The goal is to better understand when and how agent collusion happens, what evidence supports those conclusions, and how these findings could help improve future agent safety evaluations.

Matthew Southerby

Research physicist at UCL Hospitals · Independent researcher

Technical
Applying Activation Verbalisers to assess model belief Can a base model be fine-tuned to read an instruct-tuned model's beliefs from its activations?

The problem. This project attempts to answer whether a base model can be fine-tuned on model activations from the instruct-tuned model to produce a reliable Activation Verbaliser (AV) with respect to agent belief. The main questions are:

  1. How well can the base model recover concepts from the instruct-tuned model?
  2. Does fine-tuning the base model on synthetic data relating to an agent's belief translate to out-of-distribution scenarios and accurately trace the agent's belief?
  3. How well does this belief reader generalise to tricky cases (e.g. theory of mind)?

Sidar Aslanoglu

SPAR fellow

Conceptual
Threat modelling a war over Taiwan Build scenarios for a Chinese move against Taiwan and analyse what each one would do to AI chips and to compute governance.

The problem. Almost all of the world's cutting-edge chips are made in Taiwan. A blockade, a "quarantine" or an invasion would change both the pace of AI capabilities development and what an international compute governance regime can achieve. This project models such scenarios. It takes a net assessment approach to scenario modelling: laying out each side's incentives and capabilities, stating the assumptions before any conclusions, and building a small number of scenarios instead of a single probability. For each scenario, the fellow traces what happens to the supply of advanced chips and to the race between the US and China.

First two weeks. Read the open wargaming and net assessment work on a Taiwan conflict, starting with the CSIS report The First Battle of the Next War (2023), and the reporting on where leading-edge chips are made. Pick two or three scenarios and write down the assumptions behind each one before modelling anything.

You'll need. Context in AI safety or in international security, and at least one finished piece of analytical writing, of any length.

Commitment. ~4 hrs/week for 4 weeks  ·  Write-up. A post setting out scenarios and what each would mean for AI chips and compute governance

Abeer Sharma

Co-founder of AI Safety Hong Kong

Conceptual
Middle power AI governance How countries that aren't building frontier models compete in AI, and how their contexts shape how they govern it.

The problem. AI safety is often discussed in terms of frontier models, but how AI is deployed and how it diffuses matters just as much. Most countries are not building frontier models, yet they are still racing to build AI capabilities in other ways, through adoption, infrastructure, and talent, while trying to carve out a niche and capture value from the AI supply chain.

This project facilitates fellow-led research into these less discussed questions, with attention to how the distinct traits and contexts of different regions shape how AI is governed. Fellows can choose their own angle, and work can be comparative, conceptual, or policy-oriented.

Jeanne Vincendeau

Researcher in political violence and AI governance

Conceptual
The Taiwan question: forecasting and contending perspectives Map the actors, perspectives and AI dependencies behind tensions over Taiwan, and build an uncertainty-aware forecast.

The problem. Develop a grounded, uncertainty-aware assessment of the AI × Taiwan question by mapping the key actors, their capabilities and interests, and the historical events shaping current tensions. Use security analysis and forecasting methods to study how Chinese, US and Taiwanese perspectives shape interpretations and perceptions of risk, and find out how AI capabilities and dependencies affect security and escalation dynamics.

The work.

  • Disentangle facts: the landscape, actors, capabilities, interests, resources, dependencies and sources of mistrust; historical turning points, current developments and key uncertainties; AI-related strategic dependencies such as semiconductor supply chains and military AI applications.
  • Perspectives: review opinion and attitudes in mainland China, Taiwan and the United States, and compare competing narratives, interpretations and assumptions about evidence and legitimacy.
  • A pluralist, grounded forecast: assess escalation timelines and the likelihood of different scenarios, including maritime and air warfare, cyberattacks and nuclear escalation. Track uncertainty honestly: identify key indicators, pressure-test assumptions, and expose findings to local populations.

You'll need. Experience in, or an interest in learning, forecasting under uncertainty, security concepts and literature, and the context of Sino-US AI geopolitics. Knowledge of Chinese politics, culture and perspectives is a plus, as is openness to discussing epistemic incommensurability, hermeneutics and strategic narratives.

About Jeanne. Jeanne is an interdisciplinary researcher at the intersection of political violence and AI governance, with recent projects on Sino-Western relations, concentration of power, and protest repression. Their central interest is sovereignty: how autonomy can be eroded or protected under different information systems, or when AI enters people's decisions, institutions and lives.

Ina Hönemann

Physicist, Science communicator · Independent researcher

Conceptual
What safety taxes should we ask labs to pay during a slowdown? Research and evaluate concrete safety measures labs could commit to during a slowdown, and make your own recommendation.

The problem. Suppose we reach a point where frontier AI labs agree (or are forced) to slow down. What should labs actually stop doing or do differently? The central questions of this project are: what could concrete safety taxes look like? How can we evaluate and compare the options? What is your personal recommendation, and why?

A possible approach. Familiarise yourself with the literature to draft a list of possible "safety taxes": changes to how frontier models are trained and deployed that come at a cost to capabilities, speed or revenue. Options may include:

  • shifting back toward mostly-pretraining regimes
  • training only on environments known to be correct and unhackable
  • pausing training for research-engineering tasks
  • stricter sandboxing and multi-agent isolation

Analyse the likely effects, potential impact and feasibility of your proposed options. You may find it helpful to consider various perspectives, establish an evaluation framework, or test whether options hold up across different scenarios. Ultimately, you will summarise your findings and give a well-informed personal recommendation, written so that policymakers and the public can follow it.

You'll need. A basic understanding of how frontier models are trained, a desire to investigate a problem thoroughly to come to a well-reasoned conclusion, and the ability to write clearly. Some knowledge of the policy and competitive pressures AI labs face is helpful.

Commitment. ~5 hrs/week for 4 weeks  ·  Write-up. Well-argued blog post

Sudhiksha Kandavel Rajan

Research Fellow at Heron · AI safety research fellow at MIT AI Alignment

Technical
Does the reasoning match the work? Reasoning vs whole-trace monitorability Test whether an agent's stated reasoning actually reflects what it did.

The problem. When we monitor an agent by reading its reasoning, we're trusting that the reasoning reflects what the agent actually did. This project tests that assumption. The question isn't mainly about catching malicious agents; it's that even a well-behaved agent's stated reasoning can diverge from its actual behaviour: it reasons one way and acts slightly differently, or the trace contains steps the reasoning never mentions. If reasoning-monitoring can misrepresent ordinary behaviour, that limits how far we can trust it as a signal.

Travis Kim

SPAR fellow · Northwestern University

Technical
Latent structure of agentic alignment evals Do agentic alignment evals measure one general "alignment" factor, or several distinct ones?

The problem. Most alignment evaluations still use single-turn prompts or short conversations scored by an LLM judge, but as models take on agentic tasks, what they do matters more than what they say. This project measures concrete outcomes across agentic eval suites, such as Anthropic's agentic misalignment scenarios, Apollo's in-context scheming evals, SHADE-Arena and Palisade's shutdown-resistance tests, across many models. It then uses factor analysis or item response theory to see whether the results cluster into one general "alignment" factor or several distinct types.

Earlier studies found this kind of structure in chat scenarios and static benchmarks, but no one has yet looked for it in agentic, long-horizon behaviour. Working bottom-up from the evals shows which principles of alignment they really capture, and which behaviours fit none of them.

Read Travis's full project write-up for the background, proposed next steps, and risks.

You'll need. Any one of: agentic eval engineering (Inspect, sandboxes, batch APIs); psychometrics or statistics (factor analysis, IRT, multilevel models); or an AI safety background in scheming, reward hacking or sabotage evals.

Allen Hart

Research associate at the University of Warwick

Technical
Do models perceive exfiltrating their weights, successionism, or self-sustaining AI civilisations as good? Look for a "morality feature" inside models, and at how they reason about catastrophically misaligned actions.

The problem. Taking a similar perspective to Anthropic's emotions paper and the pain axis paper, this project investigates how models consider ethical or moral questions. Is there a morality feature like the pain feature? We can use techniques from mechanistic interpretability as well as chain-of-thought substitution to investigate this.

Allen is particularly interested in how models reason about scenarios where they may take catastrophically misaligned actions, like exfiltrating their own weights or sabotaging research efforts.

Medha Srivastava

Research assistant at Boston University

Conceptual
Does open AI safety research feed the automation of AI research itself? Could publishing safety research openly give new models the training data to get around those same safety measures?

The problem. One current blocker in AI safety is faithfulness: we detect misaligned behaviour partly by checking whether a model's stated reasoning reflects what it's actually doing. That research is often published openly, including the specific signals and probes used to catch unfaithful reasoning. Does publishing that research openly by default give new models the training data to bypass those very safety measures?

This project would look at which subfields are most dual-use here, since interpretability seems more exposed than something like control, and at what follows for publication norms if the effect turns out to be real.

Possible extension. Many pause proposals keep safety research running publicly while capabilities research stops. Does that kind of pause actually slow things down?

Ishita Pal

Independent researcher

Technical
When does chain-of-thought improve AI agent monitoring? How much does reading an agent's reasoning add to monitoring, beyond watching what it actually does?

The problem. As AI agents become increasingly autonomous, monitoring their behaviour is an important part of ensuring their safety. But how much additional information does an agent's chain-of-thought provide beyond its observable actions?

In this project, we will investigate whether access to an agent's reasoning improves the detection of unsafe or unauthorised behaviour compared with monitoring detailed action traces, including tool calls, outputs, and environmental changes.

Through empirical evaluations, we aim to identify when reasoning provides genuinely useful additional signals, when external observations are sufficient, and what these findings imply for designing more reliable AI control systems.

Arpita Laxmisha

MBA, University of Cambridge

Conceptual
If AI development pauses, how closely is safety research tied to capabilities research? Work out what a pause would mean for capabilities and safety research, and how safety could scale with capabilities if there is no pause.

The problem. Considering the possibility of a pause in AI development, how reasonable is it to model safety research as closely correlated to capabilities research?

Given recent incidents of AI agents going rogue, like the OpenAI Hugging Face incident and Anthropic agents breaking out of their sandbox in a UK AISI testing environment, talks of a pause have taken precedence due to fears of human extinction.

In the event of a pause, it is important to understand how it would affect capabilities research (where would the red lines be drawn?), how it would affect AI safety research in areas such as the applied methodologies needed for model evaluations, and how long the pause would need to last before it is safe to continue capabilities research. If there is no pause, it would be useful to understand how we are going to scale safety in line with capabilities.

Vincent Chang

Previously SPAR, ML4Good, TAISI and Algoverse

Technical
Extending persuasion, sycophancy and decision-making benchmarks to multilingual settings Rebuild an English-only safety benchmark in another language, taking cultural nuance into account.

The problem. Most evaluations of how language models persuade, defer to users or make decisions are run almost entirely in English. It is unclear how well these results carry over to other languages. For example, unsafe output rates are observed to rise in low-resource languages. Furthermore, safety-relevant behaviours like sycophancy and bias also affect users differently in different cultural contexts, and existing evaluations that assume an English-speaking cultural context will likely not work in other languages and cultures.

In this project, we will select a narrowly scoped benchmark and reproduce it in a new language. Most of the work is expected to be in translating the benchmark while considering cultural nuance, and validating it in a new linguistic and cultural context.

You'll need. Native fluency in a language beyond English, such that you understand its cultural nuances.

Write-up. LessWrong post

Aditya Sarna

SPAR Fellow ’26 · Google Summer of Code ’26

Technical Taking 2–4 fellows
Persistent memory and delayed cross-domain attacks in multi-agent LLM systems Measuring how delay between a memory write and its downstream effect shapes unsafe behaviour and detection.

The problem. Persistent memory in multi-agent LLM systems allows information written by one agent in one session to influence another agent much later, potentially in a seemingly unrelated domain. This challenges conventional safety evaluations, which typically inspect individual prompts, responses, or sessions in isolation. As memory accumulates and unrelated interactions intervene, unsafe behaviour may emerge far from its original cause, making its source harder to trace and existing safeguards less effective.

Why this matters.

  • Cross-agent and cross-domain risks: one agent's stored information can shape another agent's behaviour in a different task or domain.
  • Delayed activation: unsafe behaviour may emerge long after the original information was written, in a context that appears unrelated.
  • Limitations of existing safeguards: per-prompt, per-response, and per-session checks may miss risks that only emerge across time and agents.

Research focus. The project treats delay as a controlled variable, measuring how the number of intervening sessions and memory writes affects unsafe-behaviour rates and the ability of existing safeguards to detect these effects.

Scope. The project is designed to fit within the one-month fellowship. There is also an opportunity to develop the work into a research paper if fellows want to continue beyond the fellowship, but willingness to make this additional commitment will not affect selection.

Write-up. A Mercial blog post documenting the testing methodology and initial results
